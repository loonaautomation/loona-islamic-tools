Frequently Asked Questions

How many names are listed here?

All 99 names of the well-known Asma-ul-Husna list, each with its Arabic text, transliteration and a standard English meaning. Use the search box to jump to a name by its English or transliterated form.

Where does this list of 99 names come from?

It is the widely published list based on the hadith in Sunan al-Tirmidhi (3507) and Sunan Ibn Majah, reproduced in books and posters worldwide. The English meanings follow the standard published renderings.

Can I use this page for memorisation?

Yes — the grid layout, search and large Arabic text are designed for learning a few names at a time. The page works offline once loaded and nothing you do is tracked on our side.

Why do transliterations differ between websites?

Arabic sounds such as ’ayn and the emphatic letters have no exact English letters, so spelling conventions vary (for example Al-Mu’min vs Al-Mumin). The Arabic text is the reference; transliteration is a pronunciation guide.

How it works

All 99 names are printed on the page as numbered cards — the Arabic text, a transliteration and an English meaning for each — and one search box filters them live as you type. The search looks inside the transliteration and the meaning as well as the name itself, so you can find a name by what it means (try ‘provider’ or ‘king’) without knowing its Arabic form. The entire list is contained in the page; nothing is loaded from elsewhere as you scroll or search.

Worked example

Type ‘provider’ in the search box and a single card remains: number 18, Ar-Razzaq — ‘The Provider’. Clear the search and the first three cards read Ar-Rahman (The Most Gracious), Ar-Rahim (The Most Merciful) and Al-Malik (The King, the Sovereign).

Frequently asked questions

Is there any audio or a pronunciation guide for the names?

There is no audio on this page. The transliteration printed under each Arabic name is the pronunciation guide — it is written in simple Latin letters, with doubled letters and hyphens showing where a sound is held, so each name can be sounded out without knowing Arabic.

Ar-Rahman and Ar-Rahim look almost the same — what is the difference?

They share one Arabic root (r-h-m, mercy) and are traditionally explained as two shades of it: Ar-Rahman, ‘The Most Gracious’, describes mercy that embraces all of creation, while Ar-Rahim, ‘The Most Merciful’, is explained as mercy directed especially towards the believers. Scholars phrase the distinction in more than one way; the card meanings follow the most common explanation.

Are these all of the names of Allah?

These are the famous 99 of the well-known enumeration that is traditionally memorised and printed. Muslim scholars note that the names of Allah are not limited to these 99 — the list on this page is that traditional enumeration, presented in its familiar order.

Can I give one of these names to a child?

Naming customs differ between families, schools and cultures: many of these names are used with the prefix ‘Abdul-’ (‘servant of’), and some are considered appropriate for Allah alone. Because this is a religious ruling, please ask a local scholar or your family elders before deciding on a name.

Does the page work without an internet connection?

Yes, once it has loaded — all 99 cards, including the Arabic text and meanings, are part of the page itself, so reading and searching keep working with no connection at all.
